The Dutch book argument

Price your beliefs badly
and you lose, whatever happens.

Treat each of your degrees of belief as a price: what you would pay for a bet that returns $1 if you turn out to be right. If those prices break the rules of probability, a bookie can offer you a set of bets, each one fair by your own lights, that together lose you money in every possible outcome. That set of bets is called a Dutch book. If your prices obey the rules, no such set exists.

This is the classic argument that rational degrees of belief must be probabilities. Three figures you can play with come first, to build intuition. The math follows.

If your prices obey the rules of probability, there is no Dutch book

Suppose your prices obey all four rules. Then every "net" entry in the table above is zero, so those trades gain the bookie nothing. But a bookie is free to try any combination of bets, so we need an argument that covers them all at once.

Weigh the outcomes by your own credences. List the possible outcomes , the complete ways things could turn out, exactly one of which will happen. For a three-horse race there are three: "Aster wins," "Bolt wins," and "Comet wins." If the bets concern rain and wind tomorrow there are four: rain with wind, rain without wind, wind without rain, and neither. An event such as "it rains" is then true in some of the outcomes (here the first two) and false in the rest.

Any set of bets. The net gain of a set of bets is the sum of the net gains of its members, so its credence-weighted sum is zero as well.

No sure loss. Now suppose some set of bets lost money in every outcome. Each of its net gains would be negative. The weights are never negative and add up to 1, so the weighted sum would be negative too. But we just showed it is zero. So no set of bets loses in every outcome.

Fine print

What the argument assumes

  • Your credences are your betting prices, and you will take either side of a bet at that price.
  • Money is what you care about, in proportion to the amount, at least for small stakes.
  • Bets you accept one at a time, you also accept together. The book only works as a package.
  • Nobody has to be out to get you. The usual reading is that the sure loss is a symptom. It shows you are giving two different values to the same thing described in two ways.

The argument goes back to Frank Ramsey (1926) and Bruno de Finetti (1937). Everything above concerns your credences at a single time. A separate argument, due to David Lewis and more contested, uses a sequence of bets to argue that you should also update by conditioning on what you learn, replacing with once you learn .
