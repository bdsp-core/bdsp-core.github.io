The secretary problem

Look at 37%.
Then leap.

You interview n candidates one at a time, in random order. After each interview you know only how that person ranks against the ones you have already seen. You must hire or pass on the spot, and a pass is final. You win only if you hire the single best of all n.

The best strategy is to pass on the first 36.8% no matter how good they look, then hire the first person who beats everyone before them. It wins 36.8% of the time, whether n is a hundred or a million. Both numbers are 1/e. Here is where they come from, in pictures.

Step 1 · Feel the problem

Play a round with ten candidates

Bar height shows where each candidate stands among those you have seen so far. The bars rescale as new people arrive. Dark bars were the best so far when they arrived.

Picking at random wins 10% of rounds. The best possible strategy for ten candidates wins 39.9%.

Two things become obvious after a few rounds. Early on you have nothing to compare against. Later on, the good ones may already be gone. Any strategy has to trade those two risks against each other.

Step 2 · The shape of any good strategy

The best candidate is the last record. Your challenge is to guess which record is the last.

First: only records are worth hiring

Call a candidate a record if they beat everyone who came before them. Here is one full line-up with true heights shown. The dashed line is the bar each newcomer has to beat.

recordlast record = best overallbelow the bar: cannot be the best- - bar to beat

Anyone below the dashed line is already beaten. And the best overall is always the last record in the line-up. So the problem boils down to: a record is in front of you, and you must predict whether it is the last one.

Second: how likely is this record to be the last one?

Give each of the 20 positions one chip. A chip stands for a 1-in-20 chance that the overall best sits there. Now suppose you have interviewed k people and the latest one is a record.

If the overall best is anywhere among the k people you have seen, it has to be this record, because this record beats all of them. So the chips from all k seen positions pile onto the record. Each unseen position keeps its single chip.

chips on the record: it is the best overallchips on unseen positions: someone better is coming
P(record at k is the best)  =  k chipsn chips  =  kn

So the rule is a cutoff

Slide k from left to right. An early record holds a short stack, and most of the chips sit on people you have not met yet. A late record holds nearly all of them. A record gets more believable the later it shows up, so a sensible rule ignores early records and accepts late ones: skip the first r candidates, then hire the first record that appears. The open question is how large r should be. (Step 6 comes back to why a single cutoff is the right form.)

Step 3 · When does a cutoff win?

You win if no record turns up between the cutoff and the best

Suppose you skip the first r candidates, and the best candidate overall, ★ (star), is at position k. There are two ways to lose.

  1. ★ is in the skipped group. You passed on them before you started hiring.
  2. ★ comes after the cutoff, but you never get that far. Some other record shows up first, between the cutoff and ★, and you hire that person.

If neither happens, the first record you meet after the cutoff is ★, and you win.

To see how often the second problem happens, take everyone you interview before ★ and pick out the strongest of them. Call that person ◆ (diamond). Where ◆ stands settles everything.

Bar height is quality, as in Step 2. ★ is the best of all. ◆ is the best of those interviewed before ★. Teal bars are the skipped group.

◆ is in the skipped group: you reach ★ and win

Everyone between the cutoff and ★ falls below ◆ (dashed line), so none of them is a record. You pass on each one, and ★ is the first record you see.

◆ is after the cutoff: you hire too early and lose

The same line-up with ◆ and one skipped person swapped. Now ◆ rises above the best of the skipped group (dashed line), which makes ◆ a record, so you hire ◆ and never meet ★. If some other record had turned up between the cutoff and ◆, you would have hired that one instead. Either way you lose.

◆ (the strongest candidate you meet before ★) is equally likely to be in any of the k−1 positions before ★. You win when ◆ lands in one of the r skipped ones.

◆ here: you reach ★◆ here: you hire too early★ best overall

So you are safe if ◆ happens to be among the first r of the k−1 candidates you meet before reaching ★ at position k. Each of those k−1 positions is equally likely, so the chance is r out of k−1.

P(win | best at k)  =  rk − 1   if k > r,   and 0 otherwise
teal slots ÷ all slots before ★
Step 4 · Average over where the best could be

You do not know where ★ is, so average over every position

Step 3 gives your chance of winning if you know ★ is at position k. In a real round you do not know where ★ is. Rather, ★ is equally likely to be at each position, so your overall chance is the average of the Step 3 answers.

Here it is for ten candidates. Each bar is one possible position of ★, and its height is the Step 3 chance of winning in that case.

★ is in the skipped group: chance 0★ is here: chance r/(k−1), from Step 3- - average of the ten bars

Slide r to see the trade. Skipping more people turns more bars into zeros, but every bar that remains gets taller. Skipping fewer leaves more bars standing, but they drop off fast.

Written as a formula, the average is the sum of the bar heights divided by the number of bars. Call it P(r): your overall chance of hiring the best candidate when you skip the first r. Start with the average itself, then put in the Step 3 formula for each bar. The skipped positions add nothing, so the second sum starts just after the cutoff.

P(r)  =  nΣk = 1 P(best at k) · P(win | best at k)
P(r) = chance of winning with cutoff r. Go through every place ★ could be: how likely ★ is to be there, times your chance of winning if it is
P(r)  =  1n nΣk = 1 P(win | best at k)
★ is equally likely to be at each of the n positions, so P(best at k) = 1/n for every k. That makes this an ordinary average
P(r)  =  1n nΣk = r+1 rk − 1
put in the Step 3 formula: positions 1 to r are skipped and add 0, and each later position adds r/(k−1)

With many candidates the bars become a curve, and the average becomes x · ln(1/x)

Here are the same bars as Step 4, now with a slider for the number of candidates. As n grows the bars get thinner, and their tops trace out a smooth curve.

Now measure positions as fractions of the way through the line-up. As a fraction, the cutoff is at x = r/n, and ★ comes after a fraction t = (k−1)/n of the line-up. Divide the top and bottom of the bar height by n and it is written in those fractions:

rk − 1  =  r / n(k − 1) / n  =  xt
same bar height, with r replaced by x = r/n and k−1 replaced by t = (k−1)/n

The bar height x/t is the dashed curve, and the average becomes the area under that curve from the cutoff to the end.

P(x)  =  1∫x xt dt  =  x · ln1x  =  −x ln x
the area under 1/t from x to 1 is ln(1/x); the factor x scales it

Now plot that win probability against the cutoff, together with the two ways of losing. Every round ends in one of three ways, so the three bands always fill the box.

hired the best: −x ln xbest was skipped: xhired too early: the rest

The short line through the dot is the tangent. While it tilts up, skipping a little more still helps. The peak is where it lies flat.

Flat tangent means zero derivative:

P′(x)  =  −ln x − 1  =  0   ⟹   ln x = −1   ⟹   x = 1e ≈ 0.368
P(1/e)  =  1e · ln e  =  1e ≈ 36.8%
Step 6 · Why the cutoff and the win rate are the same number

Hire at the moment a record is worth as much as the future

Suppose you are at fraction x of the way through, facing a record. You have two options, and Steps 2 and 5 already priced both.

Hire now. A record at x is the best with probability x (Step 2). Pass and take the next record. That is the cutoff rule started from here, which wins with probability −x ln x (Step 5).

The figure below plots both options against how far through the line-up you are. The horizontal axis is x, your position as a fraction from 0 (the start) to 1 (the end). The vertical axis is your chance of ending up with the best candidate.

  • The straight crimson line is "hire now." It climbs steadily, because a record is more believable the later it appears.
  • The black curve is "pass and take the next record." It is the same hump as in Step 5. Early on it is low, because the next record will probably turn up too soon to be the best. Near the end it drops to zero, because there may be no next record at all.
  • Whichever is higher is the better choice at that point. In the teal region the curve is on top, so passing wins. In the amber region the line is on top, so hiring wins.
  • The dot is where they cross. To its left you should pass on every record. To its right you should hire the first one you see.
line: hire this record now, x━ curve: pass and take the next record, −x ln xwaiting is betterhiring is better

If you have met decision analysis, this is a one-way sensitivity analysis. There are two strategies, hire now and pass. The payoff of each, your chance of getting the best candidate, is plotted against your position x. At every x the better strategy is the one whose line is higher, and the point where the lines cross is the threshold at which the best choice switches.

Early on, the future is worth more than any record in hand, so you keep looking. The lines cross where the two are equal:

x  =  −x ln x   ⟹   ln x = −1   ⟹   x = 1e

The crossing point sits on the line y = x, so its height equals its position. That is why the fraction you skip and the chance you win are the same number.

Fine print

Assumptions

  • You know n in advance, and the order is random.
  • You learn only relative ranks. If you could see scores on a known scale, you could do better.
  • Only the best counts as a win. If second best were nearly as good, the right cutoff would be earlier.
  • No going back to someone you passed on.

For finite n the best cutoff is the whole number that maximizes the Step 4 sum. It is close to n/e: skip 3 of 10, 7 of 20, 37 of 100.
